Localize before disease label DEEP EXPLANATION A neurologic deficit is first an anatomy problem: cortex/subcortex, brainstem, spinal cord, peripheral nerve/root, neuromuscular junction or muscle. Side, cranial-nerve

    findings, sensory level, reflexes and cortical signs sharply narrow the differential before any disease name is needed.

    WHY STUDENTS MISS IT Students recognize familiar diagnoses from one clue and then force the rest of the examination to fit, especially in stroke and weakness vignettes. EASIEST CLUE State level + side + modality before the diagnosis. If you cannot, you are not yet ready to name the disorder. — 5. Tempo is a mechanism clue, not decorative history DEEP EXPLANATION Seconds-to-minutes suggests vascular/electrical mechanisms; hours-to-days raises inflammatory, infectious, toxic/metabolic or evolving structural processes; weeks-to-years

    favors degenerative, neoplastic or chronic compressive disease. Tempo does not make the diagnosis alone, but it changes the plausible mechanism set and urgency.

    WHY STUDENTS MISS IT Learners underweight onset because the stem contains more vivid exam findings or imaging clues. EASIEST CLUE After localization, ask how fast? Sudden + focal should push vascular/electrical causes high; slow progression should not. — 6. Weakness is a localization problem: UMN vs LMN/peripheral vs NMJ vs muscle DEEP EXPLANATION Tone/reflexes, sensory findings, fatigability and distribution distinguish central motor tract disease from peripheral neuropathy/root disease, neuromuscular-junction disorders

    and myopathy. Respiratory or bulbar involvement can convert a localization question into an immediate safety problem.
