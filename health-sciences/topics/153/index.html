    STUDY OPERATING SYSTEM - DAILY LEARNING LOOP

    ShareXinf

    ALL YEARS | PAGE 37A - ENCODE -> RETRIEVE -> APPLY -> SPACE -> RE-TEST

    The original Study Operating System used a useful cadence: daily learning, spaced review, weekly mixed questions, end-of-block/rotation assessment and monthly analytics. This expansion keeps that backbone but makes each step operational, phase-specific and evidence-driven.

    THE CORE RULE

    Do not measure studying by hours consumed. Measure whether knowledge can be produced without cues, applied to an unfamiliar vignette, retained after delay, and repaired when performance data reveal a recurring weakness.

    A. THE DAILY MASTERY LOOP

    StageActionEvidence that the stage worked
    1. OrientDefine the school objective, patient problem or exam domain. Decide what must be explainable by the end.A concrete output exists: mechanism, illness script, procedure sequence, image set or decision rule.
    2. BuildUse one strong teaching source; connect mechanism -> findings -> test -> management rather than collecting disconnected facts.Can explain the idea in your own words and draw/organize it without copying.
    3. RetrieveClose the source. Free-recall, teach aloud, draw from memory, answer flashcards or solve a short closed-book prompt.Can generate the answer before seeing cues; weak steps become visible.
    4. ApplyDo targeted or mixed questions / practical images / cases appropriate to the phase.Can transfer the concept to a new representation or vignette.
    5. ReviewReview wrong, guessed, changed and low-confidence correct answers; tag the failure mechanism.Each important miss produces one repair rule, not pages of copied explanation.
    6. Re-testReturn to a NEW item/image/case after a delay.The same error does not recur when surface details change.

    B. TOP 10 MOST-MISSED STUDY-SYSTEM CONCEPTS - 1 TO 5

    ConceptWhat it really meansWhy students miss itEasiest clue
    1. Retrieval > familiarityLearning becomes durable when you must produce the information without the original cue. Rereading can help build a model, but it does not prove retrievability.Fluent rereading feels easy and familiar, so familiarity is mistaken for mastery.Close it and produce it. If the source must stay open, you are still encoding.
    2. Spacing > crammingRevisit important material across separated sessions so retrieval occurs after some forgetting. The delay makes retrieval effortful enough to strengthen access.Cramming boosts immediate performance, which can look like learning even when retention decays quickly.Return after some forgetting. Same-night repetition alone is not spacing.
    3. Questions are learning toolsQuestions expose retrieval, discrimination and sequencing gaps. Their main value is the feedback loop, not the percentage alone.Learners postpone questions until content feels "finished" or rush explanations to protect volume.Every miss becomes a rule + new item.
    4. Interleaving trains selectionMix related problem types once a basic schema exists so you must decide which concept or algorithm applies.Blocked practice feels smoother, while interleaving feels harder and therefore falsely feels less effective.If choosing the rule is the challenge, mix the rules.
    5. Recognition is not recallSeeing an answer and thinking "I knew that" is weaker than generating the answer before the cue appears.Multiple-choice options, highlighted notes and card fronts create strong recognition cues.Blank page before cue. Predict before revealing the answer.
