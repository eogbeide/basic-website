Full Role Guide — What This Role Actually Does, Tools, Interview Questions & FAQ

A Data Analyst answers questions a company didn't know it needed to ask until the data made it obvious. At Amazon BI/Data Analyst level and comparable roles, the job isn't “make a dashboard” — it's owning a business question end to end: tracking down where the real numbers live (rarely where the ticket says), deciding what a fair comparison actually looks like, writing the SQL to prove it, and then defending the answer to a room of people who disagree with it.

The skill that separates a junior analyst from a senior one isn't SQL syntax — most job-ready candidates can already write a join. It's judgment: knowing when a metric is misleading because of survivorship bias, seasonality or a broken instrumentation event; knowing when “the data shows X” is really “the data shows X, but only for the 20% of users who did Y first.” Advanced analytics roles increasingly expect comfort with Python or R for anything a spreadsheet or BI tool can't do cleanly — cohort analysis, light statistical testing, or automating a report that used to take a day to rebuild every week.

This path is built around that judgment gap. Each SQL/analytics skill is paired with an explicit discussion of when not to trust it, and the capstone forces you to defend a “decision-grade” analysis the way you'd have to in a real business review — not just present a chart, but state what decision it should change and what would make you wrong.

Tools of the Trade

  • SQL (the real skill, not just the syntax)
  • A BI tool: Looker, Tableau, or a QuickSight-style tool
  • Python or R for anything beyond spreadsheet reach (pandas, basic stats)
  • Excel/Sheets for fast iteration and stakeholder-facing summaries
  • A shared metrics layer or semantic model, so numbers don't drift between teams

Typical Interview Questions

  • A metric went up 20% overnight. Walk me through how you'd figure out if that's real.
  • How do you decide whether a change in a metric is signal or noise?
  • Write a SQL query to find customers who churned in the month after a price change.
  • Tell me about a time your analysis contradicted what a stakeholder wanted to hear. What did you do?
  • How do you handle a dataset you don't trust?
  • What makes a dashboard actually get used, versus quietly ignored after week one?

How to Know You’re Ready

  • You can write a SQL query with multiple joins and window functions without looking up syntax every five minutes.
  • You've caught at least one metric that was wrong because of how it was instrumented, not how it was calculated.
  • You can explain a confidence interval to someone who doesn't want to hear the word “statistics.”
  • You've had an analysis change an actual decision, not just get nodded at in a meeting.

FAQ

Do I need a degree in statistics or a related field?

No. Working analysts come from a mix of backgrounds. What matters is fluency with SQL, comfort translating a vague business question into a specific query, and enough statistical literacy to avoid basic mistakes like confusing correlation with causation or ignoring sample size.

Data Analyst vs. Data Scientist — what's the real difference?

In practice, Data Analysts answer defined business questions with existing data and tools; Data Scientists more often build the predictive models, experiments and causal-inference methods analysts then use. The lines blur constantly and titles are used inconsistently across companies — read the actual job description, not just the title.

Is SQL enough, or do I need Python?

SQL is non-negotiable. Python or R is increasingly expected for anything a BI tool can't do cleanly — cohort analysis, forecasting, or automating repetitive reporting. You don't need to be a software engineer, but “I can't do that in Python” is a limiting answer in most current analyst interviews.

Role-Readiness Proof / Capstone

Own a decision-grade analysis end to end: ingest messy business or product data; build a documented semantic model and data-quality checks; use advanced SQL plus Python or R where useful; define a metric dictionary and KPI tree; create an executive dashboard and narrative; design or analyze an experiment and a causal question; use generative BI or an AI assistant for exploration; then verify every AI-derived claim against governed data and deliver a recommendation with uncertainty and limitations.

Proof Rubric

  • Show query correctness and reproducibility
  • trustworthy source-of-truth metrics
  • sound statistical assumptions and uncertainty
  • clear visual hierarchy and decision-ready storytelling
  • experiment and causal validity
  • data-quality and governance evidence
  • AI-assisted analysis with traceable verification rather than blind acceptance
  • and a recommendation tied to measurable business or customer impact, including what the data cannot support
